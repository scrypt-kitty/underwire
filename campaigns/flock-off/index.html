FLOCK OFF

Cameras on poles and patrol cars now photograph nearly every passing license plate — logging where you drive, when, and building a searchable history of your movements. Here's the field guide.

Why it matters

An automated license plate reader (ALPR) is a camera that photographs every plate that passes, reads the characters, and stamps each one with a time and location. Companies like Flock Safety sell these as networks — to police departments, but also to HOAs and businesses — and link them into a nationwide lookup system. The result isn't a targeted tool aimed at a suspect; it's a dragnet that records the movements of everyone, almost all of whom are never accused of anything.

Location data is uniquely revealing: where you sleep, worship, see a doctor, protest, or spend the night. Once it's collected and searchable, it can be queried, shared, breached, or misused — and communities from coast to coast are now voting to add guardrails or rip the cameras out entirely.

Topics

the device

What an ALPR actually does

Photographs every passing plate, OCRs the characters, and records a timestamp, GPS location, and often a vehicle image — for all traffic, not just "hits." Each pass is one more dot on a map of your life.

the vendor

What Flock Safety is

A private company that installs ALPR camera networks and sells access by subscription. It markets to police, but also to neighborhoods and businesses — putting surveillance infrastructure in private hands.

the network

The dragnet & data-sharing

Individual cameras roll up into a searchable national network. An officer (or partner agency) can look up a plate across many jurisdictions at once — building a "pattern of life" without a warrant.

the reach

Who's watching

Police departments, sheriffs, HOAs, and private security — sometimes installed with no public vote, no written policy, and no independent audit of who searches the data or why.

Debunking

"It only catches criminals."

The pitch.

ALPRs record every vehicle, overwhelmingly people who've done nothing. The system is built on mass collection first, suspicion second — the opposite of a targeted investigation.

"If you have nothing to hide, you have nothing to fear."

The pitch.

Where you drive exposes your doctor, your place of worship, a gun shop, a protest, a clinic, an affair, a support group. A permanent, searchable movement log is power over you — regardless of whether you've broken any law.

"It's no different from a cop seeing your plate on the street."

The pitch.

Scale changes the thing. A human officer forgets; a network remembers everything, forever, everywhere at once, and makes it instantly searchable and shareable across agencies. Automation of a small power creates a categorically larger one.

"The data is secure and deleted quickly."

The pitch.

Retention varies widely and is often longer than advertised; access is broad; audit logs are frequently weak or absent; and networks have shared data with hundreds of outside agencies. "Trust us" is not a security model — verifiable policy and independent audits are.

FAQ

Is ALPR legal?

Generally yes, and largely unregulated at the federal level — which is the problem. Some states and localities restrict retention, sharing, or use; rules vary a lot by place. "Legal" and "accountable" are not the same thing.

Can I opt out?

Not individually — the cameras capture public roads. The real "opt-out" is collective: policy limits or removal, decided by your city or county.

Is my town using Flock or ALPR?

Check the crowd-sourced map at deflock.org, file a public-records request with your police department, and watch council/board agendas for camera contracts.

What do they collect, and for how long?

Plate characters, a timestamp, GPS location, and usually a vehicle image — for all traffic. Retention is set by policy (often around a month, sometimes far longer). Ask your locality for its written retention schedule.

Who can access it?

Local officers, and frequently a wide network of partner agencies via lookup. Whether searches are logged, reviewed, or ever audited depends entirely on local policy — which is often missing.

What can a community actually do?

Plenty — from strict retention limits and bans on data-sharing to mandatory public audits, or removing the cameras outright. Towns are doing all of these right now.

Take action

You don't need to be a lawyer or an engineer. Start here:

  1. Map it. Find cameras near you on the deflock.org crowd map — and add ones it's missing.
  2. Request the records. Ask your police department for its ALPR policy, retention schedule, data-sharing agreements, and search audit logs.
  3. Show up. Speak during public comment at a city or county meeting. Demand a public vote and a written, audited policy before any camera goes up.
  4. Ask for real guardrails: short retention, no interstate/federal data-sharing, published audits, and a sunset review — or removal.
