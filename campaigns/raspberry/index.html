PROJECT RASPBERRY

Hyperscale AI and data-center campuses drink millions of gallons of water and draw the power of a small city — and they often arrive quietly, behind code names and shell companies. A general-public brief.

Independent education. A general-public brief from public reporting and records only. The deep, locally-sourced work on the Botetourt County case is done by community groups like SWVA DCTA and EAST — go to them for the receipts. Not legal advice.

Why it matters

A hyperscale data center is a warehouse of computers the size of a small town's worth of buildings. Running and cooling it takes staggering amounts of electricity and, frequently, water — resources a rural county may not have to spare. The public benefits are often oversold (few permanent jobs, generous tax breaks) while the costs — infrastructure upgrades, higher demand on the grid and watershed, drought risk — can land on residents and ratepayers.

"Project Raspberry" is the developer code name reported for a Google-linked campus advanced through a shell entity ("Helio") at Greenfield in Botetourt County, Virginia. The pattern there — secrecy, NDAs, and water drawn from a shared reservoir — is the same one playing out in communities across the country, which is exactly why it's worth understanding in general terms.

Topics

the basics

What a hyperscale data center is

An industrial campus of servers — hundreds of thousands of square feet — running 24/7 for cloud and AI. Think heavy industry, not an office park.

water & power

What it actually consumes

Cooling can require large, sustained water withdrawals; electricity demand can rival a small city's. The local questions: where does it come from, and who pays for the upgrades?

the playbook

Secrecy by design

Code names, shell companies, and NDAs signed by local officials before residents are told. When a "normal development" needs that much secrecy, the secrecy is the story.

the local case

Botetourt County, VA

A Google-linked campus at Greenfield, advanced via a shell entity, with public concern over water drawn from the regional supply, grid upgrades, and tax incentives. Reported by regional press.

Debunking

"It'll bring lots of local jobs."

The pitch.

Hyperscale data centers are highly automated. Construction jobs are temporary, and permanent on-site staff is typically a few dozen — a small number for the water, power, and incentives involved.

"Data centers are clean and quiet."

The pitch.

Industrial cooling systems, rows of backup diesel generators, and round-the-clock operation carry real noise, air, and water impacts for neighbors — this is industrial land use, not a quiet neighbor.

"Opposing this means you're anti-technology."

The pitch.

Wanting transparency, protected water, and a fair deal for the county isn't being anti-technology. You can use the cloud every day and still ask who pays and who decides.

"It's just a normal economic-development project."

The pitch.

Normal developments don't require public officials to sign non-disclosure agreements before residents learn the project exists. The process itself is the red flag.

FAQ

What is being built, and where?

Reporting describes a Google-linked hyperscale data-center campus at Greenfield in Botetourt County, Virginia, advanced under the code name "Project Raspberry" through a shell entity.

Why does it matter for a rural county?

Because the pressure lands locally — on the watershed, the power grid, road and utility infrastructure, and ratepayers — while the profits leave. Scale that a city might absorb can strain a smaller community.

Who actually benefits?

The company, and whoever brokered the incentives. Tax breaks and public infrastructure upgrades are common; independent analysts often find the promised local returns overstated.

How do these decisions get made?

Usually local land-use and rezoning votes (a county Board of Supervisors and Planning Commission), plus state environmental permits (in Virginia, DEQ) and — where wetlands or streams are involved — federal review (the Army Corps of Engineers). Each has a public-comment step.

Isn't this just NIMBYism?

It's about who pays and whether the process is honest. Asking for real water numbers, ratepayer protections, and an open process is civic due diligence, not a reflex.

How do I get involved locally?

Connect with the community groups already on it — SWVA DCTA (bocodc.info) and EAST — attend meetings, and submit public comment when a window opens.

Take action

The record is built from public comment. Here's how to add to it:

  1. Show up. Attend and speak at Board of Supervisors, Planning Commission, and permit hearings — in-person comment carries weight.
  2. Comment on the permits. When a state (DEQ) or federal (Army Corps) comment window opens, write two to four sentences in your own words. One personal reason is the most effective part.
  3. Use public records. Anyone can file a Freedom of Information Act request for meeting materials, agreements, and correspondence.
  4. Plug into local groups already doing the sourced work, and share what you learn with neighbors.
